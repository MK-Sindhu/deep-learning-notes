The brain analogy: how you recognize a camera

Krish connects the three layers to how he recognizes the camera in front of him while teaching.

  • Input. He sees the camera and the monitor. That signal enters through his eyes, so the eyes are the input layer.
  • Hidden layer. The signal goes on to neurons. A biological neuron does some kind of signal processing, so the hidden-layer neurons pre-process the data they receive 28:09.
  • Output. Finally the signal reaches the part of the brain, somewhere at the back, that is responsible for seeing. This is the output layer, with an output neuron that decides what the object is: a camera, a television, a mobile phone or something else.

Not in the video: the area at the back of the brain is the visual cortex, in the occipital lobe. Lecture 2 describes its layered organization (lecture-02.html).

A network has to be trained, and it can grow deeper

The analogy has a second half. When a baby sees a camera for the first time, it cannot tell that it is a camera. Someone has to teach it, again and again: this is a camera, this is food, this is your bed, this is milk. After a couple of months the baby understands. Krish's son, then about seven months old, recognizes a mobile phone and knows what his milk bottle looks like, and starts crying as soon as he sees the bottle. He learned this because he has been trained on these things from day one.

A neural network created from scratch is the same. It must always be trained, on input data together with the correct outputs. Shown a mobile phone, it should learn to predict "mobile phone"; shown a camera, "camera". Krish notes that the training process itself is a separate topic, which the following chapters take up (Part 2).

Finally, a network is not limited to one hidden layer 30:12. In the brain the signal passes through many neurons, from one to the next, and a network can likewise have any number of hidden layers, each with its own number of neurons. Hidden layer 1 might have five to ten neurons and the next layer a hundred. The right panel of the perceptron figure shows the idea.

30:27 next chapter: weights and bias

Preview: what one neuron computes

Not in the video (this part): the part ends before saying what the hidden neuron computes. The next chapter adds a weight to every connection and a bias, and Part 4 covers activation functions. This section gives the standard form so that the interactive figure can show it.

In the classic perceptron, each input \(x_i\) is multiplied by a weight \(w_i\), the products are added together with a bias \(b\), and a step function turns the sum into a 0 or 1 decision. With two inputs, say study hours \(x_1\) and play hours \(x_2\):

\[ z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b, \qquad y = \begin{cases} 1 \ (\text{pass}) & \text{if } z \ge 0 \\ 0 \ (\text{fail}) & \text{if } z < 0 \end{cases} \]

The inputs where \(z = 0\) form the line \(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b = 0\), the decision boundary. Every student on one side is predicted to pass and every student on the other side to fail. The weights tilt the line and the bias shifts it. A positive weight on study hours means more study pushes towards pass; a negative weight on play hours means more play pushes towards fail. Some sources fire the step only when \(z > 0\); the two conventions differ only for points exactly on the line.

Try the starting weights first. With \(w_1 = w_2 = 0.5\) and \(b = -3\), playing counts as much as studying, so R2 (2 hours of study, 5 of play) gets \(z = 1 + 2.5 - 3 = 0.5 \ge 0\) and is wrongly predicted to pass. Making \(w_2\) negative fixes it. For the IIT Madras treatment of weights, the bias as \(w_0 = -\theta\) and the perceptron learning algorithm that finds such a line automatically, see Lecture 2 (lecture-02.html).

Key terms

Artificial intelligence (AI)
In the video's definition, applications that do their task without human intervention, deciding from the user's behaviour. Examples: recommendations, self-driving cars, chatbots.
AI application
Existing software with an AI module integrated into it, such as the Netflix app with its recommender. The end product of every role in the field.
Machine learning (ML)
A subset of AI that provides statistical tools to analyze and visualize data, predict and forecast (supervised) and cluster (unsupervised).
Deep learning (DL)
A subset of ML built on multi-layered neural networks, with the aim of mimicking how the human brain learns. Researched since 1958.
Data science
Work that can sit at any level: building ML models, analyzing data or building DL models. Its final goal is an AI application.
Web 2.0
Web applications where users log in, store their data and interact with each other, such as Facebook. They made data grow exponentially.
Big data
Engineering for storing very large amounts of data efficiently, so that it can be read and used later. In demand from around 2008.
GPU
Graphics processing unit. It trains networks with many parameters over many epochs far faster than a CPU, and keeps getting cheaper.
Epoch
One full pass over the training data. Training usually takes many epochs. (The video uses the word without defining it.)
Perceptron
The first and simplest neural network. In the video: an input layer, a hidden layer with one neuron, and an output layer.
Input, hidden and output layers
The input layer receives one record's features, hidden layers of neurons process the signal, and the output layer gives the prediction.
Neuron
A node in a hidden or output layer that processes the signal it receives, by analogy with a biological neuron.
Binary classification
Predicting one of two classes, coded 1 and 0, such as pass and fail.
Training
Showing a network many inputs with their correct outputs so that it learns to predict them, as a baby is taught the names of things.
