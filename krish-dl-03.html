The chain rule and the vanishing gradient problem

The chain rule gives the derivative of the loss with respect to any weight as a product of local derivatives along each path from the loss back to that weight, with one product per path added up. In a deep sigmoid network every factor is small, because the sigmoid's derivative never exceeds 0.25, so the first layers get a tiny gradient and their weights barely change.

At a glance

  • Backward propagation updates every weight with \(w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \eta\,\partial L/\partial w_{\text{old}}\), and every bias the same way. The work is in finding \(\partial L/\partial w\), which the chain rule of derivatives does.
  • Follow the dependencies from the loss back to the weight and multiply one local derivative per step: \(\partial L/\partial w_4\) \(= (\partial L/\partial O_2)\,(\partial O_2/\partial w_4)\), and for a weight one layer further back, \(\partial L/\partial w_1\) \(= (\partial L/\partial O_2)\,(\partial O_2/\partial O_1)\) \(\times\, (\partial O_1/\partial w_1)\).
  • When a weight reaches the loss along several paths, compute the product for each path and add them. Krish calls this combining the paths like an OR.
  • The sigmoid \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\) has a derivative between 0 and 0.25. In a deep sigmoid network the chain for an early weight multiplies one such factor per layer, so the product gets very small.
  • A tiny gradient times a small learning rate gives \(w_{\text{new}} \approx w_{\text{old}}\): the early weights stop changing. This is the vanishing gradient problem, which he flags as a key interview question.
  • His remedy is to use other activation functions: tanh, ReLU, leaky ReLU, PReLU and others, which Part 4 covers.

What backpropagation needs: \(\partial L/\partial w\) for every weight

Part 2 (krish-dl-02.html) ended with the weight update formula of backward propagation. For any weight, such as \(w_4\),

\[ w_{4,\text{new}} = w_{4,\text{old}} - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{4,\text{old}}} \]

Everything on the right is easy to get except one piece. \(w_{4,\text{old}}\) is the current weight and the learning rate \(\eta\) is chosen at the start. The open question is how to compute \(\partial L/\partial w_{4,\text{old}}\), the derivative of the loss with respect to that weight. The tool is the chain rule of derivatives (he also calls it the chain rule of differentiation).

The running example is the small network from Part 2 1:20:19. Three inputs \(x_1, x_2, x_3\) feed one hidden neuron through the weights \(w_1, w_2, w_3\). The neuron adds its bias \(b_1\) to the weighted sum and applies an activation function; its output is \(O_1\). \(O_1\) times \(w_4\), plus a bias \(b_2\), goes into the output neuron, whose activation gives \(O_2\). That is the prediction \(\hat y\), and the loss \(L\) compares it with the true \(y\).

For the IIT Madras treatment of the same idea on a deep, thin network, see Lecture 4 (lecture-04.html).

The chain for \(w_4\), and the biases

Start from the loss and ask what it depends on. \(L\) depends first on \(O_2\), the output. \(O_2\) in turn depends on \(w_4\). One derivative per link gives

Chain rule for w₄ \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{4,\text{old}}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\cdot\frac{\partial O_2}{\partial w_{4,\text{old}}} \]

Krish checks the shape by cancelling: strike out \(\partial O_2\) above and below and \(\partial L/\partial w_4\) is left 1:21:21. A chain of derivatives connects the loss to the weight, which is where the name comes from. Each factor is the slope of one step. (Derivatives are not really fractions, so the cancelling is a way to remember the rule and check its shape. The chain rule is the theorem behind it.)

What is \(O_2\)? Call the input of the output neuron \(z\) (he calls it the "z input"). It is \(O_1\) times \(w_4\) plus the bias, and the activation is applied to it:

\[ z_2 = O_1\,w_4 + b_2, \qquad O_2 = \sigma(z_2) \]

A bias is added in every neuron: \(b_1\) in the hidden one and \(b_2\) in the output one. Biases are learned with the same update formula as the weights 1:22:55:

Bias update formula \[ b_{2,\text{new}} = b_{2,\text{old}} - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_{2,\text{old}}} \]

Not in the video: the local derivatives written out. With \(z_2 = O_1 w_4 + b_2\), the factor \(\partial O_2/\partial w_4\) is \(\sigma'(z_2)\,O_1\), and the chain for the bias is \(\partial L/\partial b_2 = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\,\sigma'(z_2)\), because \(\partial z_2/\partial b_2 = 1\). The \(\sigma'\) factor that appears here matters a great deal in the second chapter.

One layer deeper: the chains for \(w_1\) and \(w_2\)

Now update \(w_1\), the weight from \(x_1\) to the hidden neuron. The update formula has the same form, \(w_{1,\text{new}} = w_{1,\text{old}} - \eta\,\partial L/\partial w_{1,\text{old}}\), and he suggests pausing the video to write the chain yourself before he does.

The dependencies are one longer: \(L\) depends on \(O_2\), \(O_2\) depends on \(O_1\), and \(O_1\) depends on \(w_1\). So

Chain rule for w₁ \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{1,\text{old}}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\cdot\frac{\partial O_2}{\partial O_1}\cdot\frac{\partial O_1}{\partial w_{1,\text{old}}} \]

He then sets \(w_2\) as the next exercise and writes its update formula, \(w_{2,\text{new}} = w_{2,\text{old}} - \eta\,\partial L/\partial w_{2,\text{old}}\). The video moves on before writing its chain, which is the same as for \(w_1\) with the last factor changed, because \(w_2\) also enters through the hidden neuron:

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{2,\text{old}}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\cdot\frac{\partial O_2}{\partial O_1}\cdot\frac{\partial O_1}{\partial w_{2,\text{old}}} \]

A natural question is where \(w_4\) went, since it sits between \(O_1\) and \(O_2\). His answer is that any factor can be split further with the chain rule, as finely as you like, and the result is always the same derivative.

Correction. The particular split written in the video, \(\partial O_2/\partial O_1\) as \((\partial O_2/\partial w_4)\cdot(\partial w_4/\partial O_1)\), is not a valid chain-rule step, because \(w_4\) does not depend on \(O_1\). \(w_4\) is already inside the middle factor. Since \(O_2 = \sigma(O_1 w_4 + b_2)\), differentiating with respect to \(O_1\) gives \(\partial O_2/\partial O_1 = \sigma'(z_2)\,w_4\). With \(z_1 = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + w_3x_3 + b_1\) the whole chain is

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\cdot\underbrace{\sigma'(z_2)\,w_4}_{\partial O_2/\partial O_1}\cdot\underbrace{\sigma'(z_1)\,x_1}_{\partial O_1/\partial w_1} \]

So the gradient of an early weight is scaled by the weights in front of it and by one \(\sigma'\) for each neuron it passes through.

Two paths to the loss: add the products

The second example is a network with a branch. One input \(x_1\) feeds one neuron in the first hidden layer, which feeds two neurons in the second hidden layer, which both feed one output neuron. The weights are \(w_1\) (input to first hidden), \(w_2\) and \(w_3\) (first hidden to the two second-layer neurons), and \(w_4\) and \(w_5\) (those two to the output). The outputs are named by layer and neuron: \(O_{11}\) for the first hidden neuron, \(O_{21}\) and \(O_{22}\) for the second layer, \(O_{31}\) for the output neuron, which gives \(\hat y\) and then the loss.

To update \(w_1\) we need \(\partial L/\partial w_1\), and now there are two routes from \(w_1\) to the loss 1:28:53:

  • Upper path: \(L\) depends on \(O_{31}\), \(O_{31}\) on \(O_{21}\), \(O_{21}\) on \(O_{11}\), and \(O_{11}\) on \(w_1\).
  • Lower path: \(L\) depends on \(O_{31}\), \(O_{31}\) on \(O_{22}\), \(O_{22}\) on \(O_{11}\), and \(O_{11}\) on \(w_1\).

He works out the chain along the top path first, then the bottom path, and combines them "just like an OR operation": \(w_1\) influences the loss through either route, so both contributions count, and they are added.

Chain rule with two paths \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{1,\text{old}}} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial O_{31}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{31}}{\partial O_{21}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{21}}{\partial O_{11}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{11}}{\partial w_{1,\text{old}}}}_{\text{upper path}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial O_{31}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{31}}{\partial O_{22}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{22}}{\partial O_{11}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{11}}{\partial w_{1,\text{old}}}}_{\text{lower path}} \]

One product per path, then the sum over paths.

All of this happens in backward propagation 1:32:08. He says the task is done by the optimizers, which get their own chapter later (Part 5, krish-dl-05.html). More precisely, backpropagation computes the derivatives with the chain rule and the optimizer uses them in the update formula.

Not in the video: why the paths add. \(O_{31} = \sigma(w_4 O_{21} + w_5 O_{22} + b)\) depends on \(O_{11}\) through two inputs at once, and the multivariable chain rule sums the contribution of each input. Lecture 4 states this as the chain rule along several paths (lecture-04.html).

A deep network of sigmoid neurons

The second chapter is the vanishing gradient problem. Krish calls it a major problem and a very common interview question, so it is worth knowing well.

Take a very deep network with one neuron per layer: input \(x_1\), weights \(w_1, w_2, w_3, w_4\) in turn, and finally the output \(\hat y\) and the loss. The loss is the mean squared error in the form he writes here,

\[ L = \tfrac12\,(y - \hat y)^2 \]

(loss functions get a full treatment in Part 4, krish-dl-04.html). Every neuron uses the sigmoid activation, and every layer adds its own bias. The outputs of the four neurons are \(O_{21}, O_{31}, O_{41}, O_{51}\) 1:35:28, and \(O_{51}\) is \(\hat y\).

The update for the first weight is again \(w_{1,\text{new}} = w_{1,\text{old}} - \eta\,\partial L/\partial w_{1,\text{old}}\). There is one path, and it passes through every layer:

Chain rule for the first weight of a deep chain \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_{51}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{51}}{\partial O_{41}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{41}}{\partial O_{31}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{31}}{\partial O_{21}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{21}}{\partial w_1} \]

The sigmoid's derivative is at most 0.25

The sigmoid activation is

\[ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \]

Its output lies between 0 and 1. For a class label, an output above 0.5 is read as 1 and below 0.5 as 0. Drawn against its input \(z\) (here \(z = w^\top x + b\)), it is an S-shaped curve through 0.5 at \(z = 0\), with negative inputs on the left giving outputs below 0.5 and positive inputs on the right giving outputs above 0.5.

The property that matters is the size of its derivative. Whatever the input, the derivative of the sigmoid lies between 0 and 0.25. Krish writes this as a "derivative condition":

Derivative condition for the sigmoid \[ 0 \le \sigma'(z) \le 0.25 \quad \text{for every } z \]

Not in the video: why the bound is 0.25. Differentiating gives \(\sigma'(z) = e^{-z}/(1 + e^{-z})^2 = \sigma(z)\,\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big)\). This is a product of two positive numbers that add up to 1, and such a product is largest when both equal 0.5, which happens at \(z = 0\). So \(\sigma'(z) \le 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\). As \(z \to \pm\infty\), one of the two numbers goes to 0 and so does \(\sigma'\). Lecture 4 plots this derivative next to tanh's (lecture-04.html), and Lecture 7 explains how saturated sigmoid neurons kill the gradient (lecture-07.html).

Small times small: the gradient vanishes

Every \(O\) in the chain is the output of a sigmoid neuron. For example \(O_{51}\) is the sigmoid applied to \(O_{41} w_4\) plus a bias. So when backpropagation takes the derivatives with respect to \(O_{51}\), \(O_{41}\), \(O_{31}\) and the rest, each one goes through a sigmoid, and by the derivative condition each lies between 0 and 0.25.

Say one of the derivatives comes out at 0.25, the largest it can be 1:40:49. Further along the chain he names values such as 0.10, then 0.05, then 0.02: the value keeps decreasing until the end of the chain. A product of small numbers is a very small number.

  • If the four factors were 0.25, 0.10, 0.05 and 0.02, the product would be \(0.25 \times 0.10 \times 0.05 \times 0.02 = 2.5 \times 10^{-5}\).
  • Even in the best case, with every \(\sigma'\) at its maximum 0.25, \(n\) factors give \(0.25^n\): about 0.0039 for 4 layers, \(9.5 \times 10^{-7}\) for 10 layers and \(9.1 \times 10^{-13}\) for 20.

Not in the video: the weights are in the factors too. Each step between layers is \(\sigma'\) times the weight that enters it, for instance \(\partial O_{51}/\partial O_{41} = \sigma'(z_{51})\,w_4\). The bound 0.25 is on \(\sigma'\), so a factor is at most \(0.25\,|w|\). With weights of ordinary size every factor is below 1 and the product shrinks layer by layer. Very large weights can push the factors above 1, and then the product grows instead, which is the related exploding gradient problem. For the IIT Madras version of this argument, see Lecture 7 (lecture-07.html) and, for recurrent networks with the same \(\tfrac14\), Lecture 11 (lecture-11.html).

Why the weights stop changing

Now put that tiny derivative into the update formula:

\[ w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \underbrace{\eta}_{\text{small}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{\text{old}}}}_{\text{very small}} \]

The learning rate is itself a small number (Part 2 suggested values such as 0.001 or 0.01), so the change is a small number times a very small number. At some point \(w_{\text{new}} \approx w_{\text{old}}\): the weight hardly changes, or changes by a tiny amount. The weights are effectively not being updated.

The vanishing gradient problem

In a deep network with sigmoid activations, the chain-rule product for a weight near the input multiplies many derivatives that are each at most 0.25. Its gradient becomes very small, so \(w_{\text{new}} \approx w_{\text{old}}\) and that weight barely learns.

A worked example, with numbers chosen for these notes: take the four-layer chain drawn in the deep-network figure, with every weight 1, \(x_1 = 1\), biases 0 and target \(y = 1\). The forward pass gives \(\hat y = 0.660\) and \(L = 0.058\). The chain rule then gives \(\partial L/\partial w_4 = -0.051\) for the last weight but only \(\partial L/\partial w_1 = -0.00074\) for the first, about 69 times smaller after just three extra layers. With \(\eta = 0.01\), one update moves \(w_4\) from 1 to 1.00051 and \(w_1\) from 1 to 1.0000074.

Not in the video: where the damage falls. The weights near the output have short chains and keep learning normally. The weights near the input have the longest chains, so they learn far more slowly and can stay close to their random starting values. Training a deep sigmoid network then becomes very slow, even though the loss is nowhere near its minimum.

The remedy he gives: other activation functions

To solve the vanishing gradient problem, use another activation function. Krish says this is why other activation functions were developed, and lists the ones the next chapter covers:

  1. Sigmoid, the one already known.
  2. tanh.
  3. ReLU.
  4. Leaky ReLU.
  5. PReLU (parametric ReLU), and several others.

He closes by saying the next step is to see each of these and decide which is the best one to use, which is where Part 4 (krish-dl-04.html) starts.

Not in the video: the derivatives of these functions, as a preview of Part 4.

None of the alternatives caps its derivative at 0.25. tanh's derivative reaches 1 at \(z = 0\), and the ReLU family passes a derivative of exactly 1 for positive inputs, so a factor in the chain need not shrink. Key terms

Weight update formula
\(w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \eta\,\partial L/\partial w_{\text{old}}\), applied to every weight in backward propagation.
Bias update
\(b_{\text{new}} = b_{\text{old}} - \eta\,\partial L/\partial b_{\text{old}}\). Each neuron has a bias, learned the same way as the weights.
Chain rule of derivatives
The derivative of the loss with respect to a weight is the product of one local derivative per step on the way from the loss back to the weight.
Local derivative
The derivative of one step, such as \(\partial O_2/\partial O_1\) or \(\partial O_1/\partial w_1\). For a sigmoid neuron it is \(\sigma'(z)\) times the weight or input of that step.
Path
A route of dependencies from a weight to the loss. With several paths, compute each path's product and add them.
Backward propagation
Computing these derivatives from the loss back toward the input, then updating weights and biases with them.
Optimizer
The method that applies the gradients to update the weights; Krish gives optimizers their own chapter (Part 5).
Sigmoid
\(\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\). Output between 0 and 1, equal to 0.5 at \(z = 0\).
Derivative condition
\(0 \le \sigma'(z) \le 0.25\), with \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\) largest at \(z = 0\).
Mean squared error
The loss used here, \(L = \tfrac12(y - \hat y)^2\). Its derivative with respect to \(\hat y\) is \(\hat y - y\).
Deep neural network
A network with many hidden layers. The chain for a weight near the input has one factor per layer.
Vanishing gradient problem
In a deep sigmoid network the gradient for early weights is a product of many small derivatives, so it is tiny and \(w_{\text{new}} \approx w_{\text{old}}\).
tanh, ReLU, leaky ReLU, PReLU
The alternative activation functions Krish lists as the remedy; they are the topic of Part 4.

Test yourself

Q1In the 3–1–1 network, write \(\partial L/\partial w_4\) and \(\partial L/\partial w_1\) as chain-rule products. Why does the second have one more factor?

\(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_4} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\cdot\frac{\partial O_2}{\partial w_4}\) and \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\cdot\frac{\partial O_2}{\partial O_1}\cdot\frac{\partial O_1}{\partial w_1}\). \(w_4\) feeds the output neuron directly, so the loss depends on it in two steps. \(w_1\) feeds the hidden neuron, whose output \(O_1\) then feeds the output neuron, so there is one more step and one more factor.

Q2Finish the exercise from the video: write \(\partial L/\partial w_2\). Then write the update for the bias \(b_2\) and its chain.

\(w_2\) also enters through the hidden neuron, so only the last factor changes: \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\cdot\frac{\partial O_2}{\partial O_1}\cdot\frac{\partial O_1}{\partial w_2}\). The bias update is \(b_{2,\text{new}} = b_{2,\text{old}} - \eta\,\partial L/\partial b_{2,\text{old}}\), with \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\cdot\frac{\partial O_2}{\partial b_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\,\sigma'(z_2)\), since \(z_2 = O_1w_4 + b_2\) changes one for one with \(b_2\).

Q3In the 1–1–2–1 network, how many products make up \(\partial L/\partial w_1\), \(\partial L/\partial w_2\) and \(\partial L/\partial w_5\)? Write \(\partial L/\partial w_2\).

\(w_1\): two products, one through \(O_{21}\) and one through \(O_{22}\), added. \(w_2\): one, because \(w_2\) only feeds \(O_{21}\): \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_{31}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{31}}{\partial O_{21}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{21}}{\partial w_2}\). \(w_5\): one, with two factors, \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial O_{31}}\cdot\frac{\partial O_{31}}{\partial w_5}\). A weight has as many products as there are routes from it to the loss.

Q4In the 3–1–1 network, where does \(w_4\) appear in \(\partial L/\partial w_1\)?

Inside the middle factor. \(O_2 = \sigma(O_1w_4 + b_2)\), so \(\frac{\partial O_2}{\partial O_1} = \sigma'(z_2)\,w_4\), and \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial O_2}\,\sigma'(z_2)\,w_4\,\sigma'(z_1)\,x_1\). It does not need a separate factor such as \(\partial w_4/\partial O_1\); that derivative is 0, since \(w_4\) does not depend on \(O_1\).

Q5Show that \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\) and that \(0 \le \sigma'(z) \le 0.25\). Where is the maximum?

\(\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}\), so \(\sigma'(z) = e^{-z}(1 + e^{-z})^{-2}\) \(= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}\) \(= \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\). Both factors lie in \((0, 1)\) and add to 1, so the product is positive and at most \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\), reached when \(\sigma(z) = 0.5\), that is at \(z = 0\).

Q6Suppose \(\partial L/\partial w_1\) \(= 0.25 \times 0.10 \times 0.05 \times 0.02\), \(\eta = 0.01\) and \(w_{1,\text{old}} = 0.5\). What is \(w_{1,\text{new}}\)? What is the largest product of ten sigmoid derivatives?

The product is \(2.5 \times 10^{-5}\), so the step is \(0.01 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-5} = 2.5 \times 10^{-7}\) and \(w_{1,\text{new}} = 0.49999975\), practically \(w_{1,\text{old}}\). Ten sigmoid derivatives give at most \(0.25^{10} \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-7}\), and in practice less, since \(\sigma'\) is only 0.25 at \(z = 0\).

Q7Why does the vanishing gradient hit the first layers and not the last?

The number of factors in the chain grows with the distance from the loss. The last weight's gradient has one sigmoid derivative in it; the first weight's has one per layer. In the four-layer example with all weights 1, \(\partial L/\partial w_4 = -0.051\) but \(\partial L/\partial w_1 = -0.00074\), about 69 times smaller. Each added sigmoid layer multiplies the gap again, here by about \(1/0.22 \approx 4.5\).

Q8What remedy does Krish give, and why should it help?

Use another activation function: tanh, ReLU, leaky ReLU, PReLU and others, covered in Part 4. The reason (beyond what this part of the video says) is that their derivatives are not capped at 0.25. tanh's reaches 1, and ReLU's is exactly 1 for positive inputs, so a factor in the chain does not have to shrink the gradient.
