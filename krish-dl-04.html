Activation functions and loss functions

Krish compares the activation functions a neuron can use, from sigmoid and tanh to ReLU and its variants, by their formulas, derivatives, strengths and weaknesses, and gives a rule for which one goes where. He then covers the loss functions for regression (MSE, MAE, Huber) and for classification (binary and categorical cross-entropy with a softmax output), and pairs each kind of problem with an output activation and a loss.

At a glance

  • An activation function shapes each neuron's output, and its derivative enters every chain-rule product in backpropagation. Sigmoid gives outputs in \((0, 1)\) but its derivative is at most 0.25, so gradients vanish in deep networks; it is also not zero-centred and needs an exponential. tanh is zero-centred with a derivative of up to 1, but it still saturates.
  • ReLU \(= \max(0, x)\) is cheap and has derivative 1 for positive inputs, which makes it the default for hidden layers. For negative inputs its output and derivative are both 0, which can leave a dead neuron that never updates. Leaky ReLU (\(0.01x\)), PReLU (a learned slope) and ELU (\(\alpha(e^x - 1)\)) keep a non-zero gradient there. Swish is a smooth, self-gated alternative.
  • Krish's rule: ReLU in the hidden layers (PReLU or ELU if training does not converge). In the output layer use sigmoid for binary classification, softmax for multi-class classification and a linear unit for regression.
  • A loss function measures the error on one record and a cost function the error on a batch. For regression, MSE is smooth with a single minimum but outliers dominate it; MAE is robust to outliers but needs a sub-gradient at 0; Huber is squared for errors up to \(\delta\) and linear beyond.
  • For classification, binary cross-entropy (log loss) works on a sigmoid output. Categorical cross-entropy works on one-hot labels and a softmax output, which turns the output scores into probabilities that sum to 1.

Sigmoid: smooth, but its gradient vanishes

An activation function decides what a neuron passes on once it has formed the weighted sum of its inputs. Krish teaches this chapter from a written summary, which he says he will share, that shows each function on the left and its derivative on the right. The derivative matters because backpropagation multiplies one such derivative per layer in the chain rule, as Part 3 showed (krish-dl-03.html).

\[ \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}, \qquad 0 \lt \sigma(x) \lt 1, \qquad 0 \lt \sigma'(x) \le 0.25 \]

The sigmoid's output lies between 0 and 1, and its derivative between 0 and 0.25. That small derivative is the reason the vanishing gradient problem exists.

Advantages

  • Smooth gradient. The curve has no jumps, which prevents jumps in the output values. Krish adds that the smooth gradient helps training converge.
  • Normalized output. Every neuron's output lies between 0 and 1.
  • Clear predictions. Large inputs give outputs very close to 1 or 0.

Disadvantages

  1. Gradient vanishing 1:47:45. Once the input moves away from the origin, the gradient becomes very small, almost 0. Backpropagation computes each weight's gradient with the chain rule, and every sigmoid on the way contributes one of these small factors, so the product for the early weights becomes tiny. For scale (computed for these notes): \(\sigma'(0) = 0.25\), \(\sigma'(2) \approx 0.105\) and \(\sigma'(4) \approx 0.018\).
  2. Not zero-centred. Krish explains the term with a sketch. A zero-centred curve passes through the origin and produces both negative and positive values. The sigmoid passes through \((0, 0.5)\) and its outputs are always positive, so it is not zero-centred. He says weight updates are more efficient with a zero-centred function, and that this property reduces the efficiency of the sigmoid's weight updates.
  3. Slow to compute. Each evaluation needs the exponential \(e^{-x}\), which is a slow operation for a computer.

Not in the video: why zero-centring matters. The next layer receives only positive inputs, so all the gradients of the weights feeding one neuron share a sign. They can all increase or all decrease together, and gradient descent has to zigzag toward an optimum that needs some weights up and others down. For the IIT Madras treatment, see Lecture 7 (lecture-07.html).

A network whose gradients vanish cannot be made deep, so researchers kept looking for better functions 1:49:25. The rest of the chapter follows that search.

1:49:58 1:50:31 derivative 1:51:36 compared with sigmoid

tanh: zero-centred, still saturating

The second function researchers proposed is the hyperbolic tangent.

\[ \tanh(x) = \frac{e^{x} - e^{-x}}{e^{x} + e^{-x}}, \qquad -1 \lt \tanh(x) \lt 1, \qquad 0 \lt \tanh'(x) \le 1 \]

Its output ranges from \(-1\) to \(+1\), and its derivative from 0 to 1. That is better than the sigmoid's 0 to 0.25. It still does not fully prevent the vanishing gradient problem. In a very deep network the gradient can still vanish at some point, so Krish advises against tanh in the hidden layers as well. tanh does fix the centring problem. The curve passes through the origin and gives negative and positive outputs.

The summary he reads compares the two curves, which look alike. For inputs far from 0 the output flattens and the gradient is small, which hampers the weight update. (The captions say "conducive" here, but a small gradient slows updates, so "not conducive" must be meant.) The difference is the output interval, \((-1, 1)\), and the curve is zero-centred, which makes tanh better than sigmoid. The summary also gives an older convention for binary classification: tanh in the hidden layers and sigmoid in the output layer. Krish's own rule, below, replaces tanh with ReLU.

Not in the video: \(\tanh'(x) = 1 - \tanh^2(x)\), and \(\tanh(x) = 2\sigma(2x) - 1\), a sigmoid stretched to \((-1, 1)\). That is why tanh keeps the sigmoid's flat ends. For the IIT Madras treatment, see Lecture 7 (lecture-07.html).

ReLU: fast and non-saturating, but neurons can die

The next function is the most popular one today. Krish jokes that people use it even when they know nothing about why. It is the rectified linear unit.

\[ \operatorname{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x), \qquad \operatorname{ReLU}'(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x \lt 0 \\ 1 & x \gt 0 \end{cases} \]

A negative input becomes 0 and a positive input passes through unchanged: 1 gives 1, 2 gives 2, 3 gives 3. The derivative is either 0 or 1.

The dead neuron problem

The derivative of 0 is a concern. Suppose a neuron in hidden layer 1 has ReLU derivative 0 during backpropagation. The chain-rule product for every weight feeding that neuron contains this 0, so the whole gradient is 0 and

\[ w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \eta \cdot 0 = w_{\text{old}} . \]

In the forward pass the neuron outputs 0, and in the backward pass it passes no gradient. Its weights stop changing and the neuron is dead. This is ReLU's main disadvantage.

Not in the video: one negative input only skips one update. A neuron is dead for good when its pre-activation is negative for every training example, for instance after a large update pushes its bias far below 0. Then no example can revive it.

Why ReLU still wins

  • Speed. There is no exponential, only a comparison with 0, so ReLU is much quicker to compute than sigmoid and tanh.
  • No vanishing from the activation. The derivative is exactly 1 for every positive input, so ReLU does not shrink the gradient there. Krish says this solves the vanishing gradient problem and the other problems of sigmoid and tanh.
  • The remaining drawback 1:54:52. ReLU is not zero-centred. The curve passes through 0 but never gives negative values.

The summary he reads adds a few points. ReLU is not differentiable everywhere: at \(x = 0\) there is a corner, and a sub-gradient is used instead of a derivative. When the input is positive there is no gradient saturation. ReLU is linear on each side, in the forward and the backward pass alike, so it is much faster than sigmoid and tanh. Its disadvantages are that it is completely inactive for negative inputs, so a neuron that only receives negative inputs dies, and that it is not zero-centred.

Not in the video: TensorFlow and PyTorch use 0 as ReLU's derivative at exactly \(x = 0\). For the IIT Madras treatment, see Lecture 7 (lecture-07.html).

Leaky ReLU: a small slope for negative inputs

To solve the dead neuron problem, leaky ReLU changes one thing. For a negative input it outputs \(0.01x\) instead of 0.

\[ \operatorname{LeakyReLU}(x) = \max(0.01x,\ x) = \begin{cases} x & x \gt 0 \\ 0.01x & x \le 0 \end{cases}, \qquad \operatorname{LeakyReLU}'(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & x \gt 0 \\ 0.01 & x \lt 0 \end{cases} \]

The curve now dips slightly below 0 on the left, so the function has some negative outputs. More importantly, the derivative is never 0: it is 0.01 for negative inputs and 1 for positive ones. A neuron whose input is negative still gets a small update, which solves the dead neuron problem.

The summary then describes the step that leads to PReLU. Setting the left half to \(0.01x\) is one choice, and another intuitive idea is a parameter-based method that uses some other slope there. In theory leaky ReLU has all of ReLU's advantages and no dead neurons. In practice, the summary says, it has not been fully proved that leaky ReLU is always better than ReLU. People still use it as an option.

Krish promises to say which function to use by default for each kind of problem once all of them are covered. That rule is in Which one to use.

1:58:08

ELU: an exponential curve for negative inputs

The exponential linear unit keeps the identity for positive inputs and bends smoothly for negative ones.

\[ \operatorname{ELU}(x) = \begin{cases} x & x \gt 0 \\ \alpha\,(e^{x} - 1) & x \le 0 \end{cases}, \qquad \operatorname{ELU}'(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & x \gt 0 \\ \alpha\, e^{x} & x \le 0 \end{cases} \]

For negative inputs the curve flattens toward \(-\alpha\). The derivative also has an exponential part, and its values lie between 0 and 1. The summary lists three properties:

  • no dead ReLU problem, since the derivative is never 0;
  • outputs can be negative, so the mean output is close to zero and the function is close to zero-centred;
  • one small problem: it is slightly more expensive to compute, because of the exponential.

Krish calls these variants small changes to ReLU that people do use.

Not in the video: the usual choice is \(\alpha = 1\), Keras's default. With \(\alpha = 1\) both pieces have slope 1 at \(x = 0\), so ELU has no corner there and its derivative lies in \((0, 1]\).

1:58:38 1:59:11 Swish

PReLU and Swish

Softmax is the next function in the summary, but Krish postpones it to the loss functions (see Softmax) and turns to the parametric ReLU, PReLU. From the diagram alone, he says, you can see what it does. Writing \(y_i\) for the input of unit \(i\):

\[ f(y_i) = \begin{cases} y_i & y_i \gt 0 \\ a_i\, y_i & y_i \le 0 \end{cases} \]
  • If \(a_i = 0\), \(f\) is ReLU.
  • If \(a_i\) is a small fixed positive value, such as 0.01, \(f\) is leaky ReLU.
  • If \(a_i\) is a learnable parameter, \(f\) is PReLU. The slope keeps changing during training, and that is the only difference.

Not in the video: \(a_i\) is learned by gradient descent along with the weights. The index \(i\) means each unit (or channel) can learn its own slope.

The last function he shows is Swish, a self-gated function that he believes came from Google, and he shows its curve. Then he closes the list: any of these functions can be used, and what matters is a technique for choosing.

Not in the video: \(\operatorname{Swish}(x) = x\,\sigma(\beta x)\), proposed by Google Brain researchers in 2017; with \(\beta = 1\) it is also called SiLU. "Self-gated" means the input gates itself through \(\sigma(x)\). The curve is smooth, dips slightly below 0 for negative inputs (minimum about \(-0.28\) near \(x = -1.28\)) and grows like \(x\) for large positive inputs. Its derivative is \(\sigma(x) + x\,\sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))\). The captions here also mention a derivative that cannot be found at zero. That is true of ReLU's corner; Swish is differentiable everywhere. For the IIT Madras treatment, see Lecture 7 (lecture-07.html).

Which activation to use where

Krish's technique starts from what to avoid. Sigmoid in the hidden layers gives vanishing gradients, and so does tanh. The rest depends on the problem.

  • Binary classification 2:00:13. Use ReLU in every hidden layer, since it is the most efficient activation, and sigmoid in the output layer. If training does not converge with ReLU, change the hidden layers to PReLU or ELU 2:01:20. The output of a binary classifier should always be a sigmoid.
  • Multi-class classification. Use softmax in the output layer (the loss functions chapter explains it). The hidden layers again use ReLU by default, or PReLU and other variants if training does not converge. ReLU solves most of the problems. At one point 2:02:57 he also names sigmoid as an option for the multi-class output.
  • Regression, where the output is a continuous value. The hidden layers use ReLU or one of its variants, and the output layer uses a linear activation, \(f(x) = x\), so the output can take any value. Regression also has its own loss functions, which come next.

Not in the video: separate sigmoid outputs suit multi-label problems, where one example can belong to several classes at once. When each example has exactly one of \(C\) classes, softmax is the standard output.

Hidden layers: ReLU, or PReLU or ELU if training does not converge. Output layer: sigmoid for binary classification, softmax for multi-class classification, linear for regression.

Loss functions for regression and classification

Loss appears right after forward propagation: the network computes \(\hat y\), the loss measures how far it is from \(y\), and backpropagation tries to reduce it. Different problems need different loss functions. An artificial neural network solves two kinds of problem, regression and classification.

  • Regression. A data set with years of experience and degree as features and salary as the output. Someone with 10 years of experience and a PhD will have a high salary. The output is a continuous value.
  • Classification. A data set with play hours and study hours as features and pass or fail as the output. Krish's illustrative rows are below. Two outcomes make it binary classification; more categories make it multi-class.

2:08:20 2:08:53 batches 2:10:00 the difference

Loss function vs cost function

Take a data set of 100 records. One way to train is to pass one record at a time: the forward pass computes \(\hat y\), the loss \((y - \hat y)^2\) is computed, and backpropagation updates the weights. This happens for every record, which is not efficient.

Instead, set a batch size, say 10. Each forward pass then takes 10 records, and the loss is computed for all 10 together. That combined quantity is called the cost function. With \(n\) the batch size, Krish writes

Loss and cost for squared error, as Krish writes them \[ \text{loss (one record): } L = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(y - \hat y)^2, \qquad \text{cost (a batch of } n\text{): } J = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 \]

The loss function uses one data point; the cost function uses a batch of data points.

He describes each batch as being passed in every epoch. The usual terms are an iteration (or step) for one forward and backward pass over a batch, and an epoch for one pass over all the records: here, 10 iterations of 10 records. Not in the video: Keras averages the per-record losses over the batch, dividing by \(n\) instead of using \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), so the cost does not grow with the batch size.

2:10:32 2:11:36 quadratic 2:12:39 advantages 2:14:18 outliers

Mean squared error

The first regression loss is the mean squared error (MSE).

\[ L = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(y - \hat y)^2, \qquad J = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 \]

Krish calls this formula a quadratic equation. Like \((a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2\), it has the general form \(ax^2 + bx + c\), and plotting it gives a parabola: the bowl-shaped curve that gradient descent moves down, with one lowest point.

Advantages

  1. Differentiable. The curve is smooth, so its derivative exists everywhere, and the weight update formula uses that derivative.
  2. One minimum. The curve has only one minimum, which is both local and global.
  3. Fast convergence. It converges faster.

Not in the video: the single minimum is a property of the loss as a function of \(\hat y\), and of linear regression's cost as a function of its weights. A deep network's cost as a function of its weights is not a parabola and can have many minima. The \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) is there for convenience: \(\partial L / \partial \hat y = -(y - \hat y)\), with no factor 2.

Disadvantage: not robust to outliers

MSE has one disadvantage, and Krish calls it a big one. Picture a linear regression problem. Without outliers, the best-fit line follows the points. Add a few outliers and the new best-fit line shifts strongly toward them 2:14:50. The reason is the square. Each error is squared, so large errors are penalized far more than small ones: an error of 10 adds 100 to the sum while an error of 1 adds 1. A few outliers then dominate the cost, and the line moves to reduce their errors.

For the IIT Madras treatment of squared-error loss in a network, see Lecture 4 (lecture-04.html).

2:16:29 2:17:06 robust 2:17:38 sub-gradient

Mean absolute error

Researchers answered MSE's weakness with the mean absolute error (MAE), which takes the absolute value of the error instead of squaring it.

\[ L = \lvert y - \hat y \rvert, \qquad J = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lvert y_i - \hat y_i \rvert \]

Krish writes a \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) in front of both, carried over from MSE. The usual MAE has no such factor, and a constant factor does not move the minimum. Keras's version also divides by \(n\).

Because the error is not squared, a large error is not penalized out of proportion. That is MAE's major advantage: it is robust to outliers. With a few outliers the best-fit line still shifts, but only a little, as the MAE line in the figure above shows.

Plotted against the error, MAE is a V shape with a corner at 0, where it has no derivative. For the weight update Krish says we must take a sub-gradient: split the curve into its pieces and use the slope of each piece (\(-1\) on the left, \(+1\) on the right). He calls this a small task that still takes time, so MAE is more time-consuming to optimize than MSE.

Not in the video: at the corner any slope between \(-1\) and \(1\) is a valid sub-gradient, and frameworks use 0. MAE's slope stays \(\pm 1\) however small the error is, so its steps do not shrink near the minimum the way MSE's do.

2:18:41 2:19:13 the threshold δ 2:19:46 large errors

Huber loss: squared for small errors, linear for large ones

The third regression loss, Huber loss, combines MSE and MAE.

Huber loss with threshold δ \[ L_\delta(y, \hat y) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2}\,(y - \hat y)^2 & \text{if } \lvert y - \hat y \rvert \le \delta \\ \delta\,\lvert y - \hat y \rvert - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\delta^2 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

\(\delta\) is a hyperparameter, and it decides whether a point counts as an outlier. When the error is at most \(\delta\), there is no outlier and the squared branch is used, as in MSE. When the error is larger, the point is treated as an outlier and the linear branch is used, as in MAE. A good \(\delta\) is found while coding, by trying values.

Correction: the spoken second branch is garbled in the captions. The standard form is the one above, \(\delta\,(\lvert y - \hat y \rvert - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta)\). Not in the video: with it the two branches meet at \(\lvert y - \hat y \rvert = \delta\) with the same value \(\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2\) and the same slope \(\delta\), so Huber loss is differentiable everywhere. In Keras it is tf.keras.losses.Huber(delta=1.0).

With MSE, MAE and Huber, the regression losses are complete. The next losses are for classification 2:20:16.

Binary cross-entropy

Classification uses cross-entropy, in two types: binary cross-entropy for binary classification and categorical cross-entropy for multi-class classification. As before, the cost over a batch is the sum of the per-record losses over \(i = 1, \dots, n\).

Krish asks whether viewers have learned logistic regression, because its loss, the log loss, is exactly binary cross-entropy. Logistic regression is a binary classifier too.

Binary cross-entropy (log loss) \[ L = -\,y \log \hat y \;-\; (1 - y) \log (1 - \hat y) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad L = \begin{cases} -\log (1 - \hat y) & \text{if } y = 0 \\ -\log \hat y & \text{if } y = 1 \end{cases} \]

The two forms agree. When \(y = 0\), the first term is 0 and \(1 - y = 1\), which leaves \(-\log(1 - \hat y)\). When \(y = 1\), the second term is 0, which leaves \(-\log \hat y\).

How is \(\hat y\) computed? With a sigmoid activation in the last layer. The network applies the sigmoid to get \(\hat y\), applies the log loss to it, and then backpropagates.

2:24:34 2:25:10 one-hot encoding 2:25:45 formula 2:26:53 notation

Categorical cross-entropy

Categorical cross-entropy is for multi-class classification. Krish's example data set has features \(f_1, f_2, f_3\) with numeric values and an output with three categories: good, bad and neutral.

The first step is to one-hot encode the output. It becomes three columns, good, bad and neutral. In each row the column of that row's class is 1 and the others are 0.

Outputgoodbadneutral
good100
bad010
neutral001

The loss for record \(i\), with \(C\) the number of categories, is

Categorical cross-entropy \[ L(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_i) = -\sum_{j=1}^{C} y_{ij}\, \ln \hat y_{ij}, \qquad y_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if record } i \text{ is in class } j \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

The notation: \(i\) is the row (the record) and \(j\) the column (the category). Row \(i\)'s label is the one-hot vector \(\mathbf{y}_i = (y_{i1}, y_{i2}, \dots, y_{iC})\). If the record is good, \(y_{i1} = 1\) and the rest are 0. The predicted \(\hat y_{ij}\) come from another activation function in the output layer, softmax.

The captions do not include the minus sign in front; it is needed, because \(\ln \hat y_{ij} \le 0\) and the loss must be positive. Not in the video: only one \(y_{ij}\) is 1, so the sum is just \(-\ln\) of the probability given to the true class. With \(C = 2\) it reduces to binary cross-entropy.

2:28:32 2:29:05 formula 2:29:38 example 2:30:49 sigmoid vs softmax

Softmax: from output scores to probabilities

Softmax is the activation that Krish earlier placed in the output layer of a multi-class classifier. For the scores \(z_1, \dots, z_K\) arriving at the \(K\) output units,

\[ \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}} \]

His example network has three hidden neurons connected to the output units. After the weights are applied, the output units receive scores, which he calls 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50. The probability of the first class is then

\[ \frac{e^{10}}{e^{10} + e^{20} + e^{30} + e^{40} + e^{50}} . \]

Each output becomes a probability, all of them add up to 1, and the class with the highest probability is the prediction. The probabilities he quotes (0.4, 0.5, 0.6) are placeholders and do not add up to 1.

Not in the video: with his scores, softmax gives the class scored 50 a probability of about 0.99995 and the class scored 10 about \(4 \times 10^{-18}\). Exponentials turn score gaps into large probability ratios.

The figure works through a smaller illustrative example with three classes, then computes the categorical cross-entropy.

He repeats the pairing that matters for coding: softmax in the output layer for a multi-class problem, sigmoid for a binary one.

Not in the video: sparse categorical cross-entropy is the same loss with the labels given as class indices (0, 1, 2) instead of one-hot vectors. In Keras, use loss="sparse_categorical_crossentropy" with integer labels and loss="categorical_crossentropy" with one-hot labels.

Putting it together: activation and loss for each problem

from tensorflow.keras import layers

n_features, C = 3, 3   # e.g. features f1, f2, f3 and classes good, bad, neutral

# Binary classification: ReLU hidden layers, one sigmoid output, binary cross-entropy
binary = keras.Sequential([

    layers.Dense(16, activation="relu"),
    layers.Dense(8, activation="relu"),
    layers.Dense(1, activation="sigmoid"),
])

# Multi-class: C softmax outputs, categorical cross-entropy on one-hot labels
multi = keras.Sequential([
    keras.Input(shape=(n_features,)),
    layers.Dense(16, activation="relu"),
    layers.Dense(C, activation="softmax"),
])
multi.compile(optimizer="adam", loss="categorical_crossentropy", metrics=["accuracy"])
# with integer labels 0 .. C-1 instead: loss="sparse_categorical_crossentropy"

Key terms

Activation function
The function a neuron applies to its weighted sum. Its derivative enters the chain rule in backpropagation.
Zero-centred
A curve through the origin with negative and positive outputs, such as tanh. Sigmoid and ReLU are not zero-centred.
Vanishing gradient
Gradients shrink toward 0 as they pass back through layers whose activation derivatives are small, such as sigmoid's (at most 0.25).
Dead neuron
A ReLU unit with negative input: its output and derivative are 0, so its weights get no update, \(w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}}\).
Leaky ReLU and PReLU
\(x\) for \(x \gt 0\) and \(a x\) otherwise. Leaky ReLU fixes \(a = 0.01\); PReLU learns \(a\); \(a = 0\) is ReLU.
ELU
\(x\) for \(x \gt 0\), \(\alpha(e^x - 1)\) otherwise. No dead neurons, mean output near 0, but an exponential to compute.
Swish
A smooth self-gated activation from Google, \(x\,\sigma(x)\) (formula not given in the video).
Softmax
\(e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j}\): turns the output scores into probabilities that sum to 1. Output layer of a multi-class classifier.
Loss vs cost function
The loss measures the error on one record; the cost function adds up the losses over a batch of records.
Mean squared error (MSE)
\(\tfrac{1}{2}(y - \hat y)^2\) per record. Differentiable with one minimum and fast to converge, but not robust to outliers.
Mean absolute error (MAE)
\(\lvert y - \hat y \rvert\) per record. Robust to outliers; at its corner a sub-gradient replaces the derivative.
Huber loss
Squared for \(\lvert y - \hat y \rvert \le \delta\) and \(\delta\lvert y - \hat y \rvert - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2\) beyond; \(\delta\) is a hyperparameter.
Binary cross-entropy
Log loss, \(-y \log \hat y - (1 - y)\log(1 - \hat y)\), on a sigmoid output.
Categorical cross-entropy
\(-\sum_j y_{ij} \ln \hat y_{ij}\) with one-hot labels \(y_{ij}\) and softmax outputs \(\hat y_{ij}\).

Test yourself

Q1Each sigmoid layer contributes one derivative to the chain-rule product. What is the largest factor it can contribute, what does that give for 5 layers, and why is tanh better but not a cure?

\(\sigma'(x) \le 0.25\), reached at \(x = 0\). Five layers give at best \(0.25^5 \approx 0.00098\), about a thousandth, before the weights are counted; away from \(x = 0\) each factor is smaller still. tanh's derivative reaches 1 at \(x = 0\), so the best case does not shrink. Everywhere else it is below 1 and near 0 for large \(\lvert x \rvert\), so in a very deep network the product can still vanish.

Q2A ReLU neuron has pre-activation \(z = -1.7\). What is its output, what gradient reaches its incoming weights, and what changes with leaky ReLU?

Output \(\max(0, -1.7) = 0\). The derivative \(\operatorname{ReLU}'(-1.7) = 0\), so \(\partial L/\partial w = \partial L/\partial h \cdot 0 \cdot x = 0\) and \(w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}}\). If every input gives a negative \(z\), the neuron never changes again: it is dead. With leaky ReLU the output is \(-0.017\) and the derivative 0.01, so \(\partial L/\partial w = 0.01 \cdot \partial L/\partial h \cdot x\), a small update that lets the neuron recover.

Q3In \(f(y_i) = y_i\) for \(y_i \gt 0\) and \(a_i y_i\) otherwise, which choice of \(a_i\) gives ReLU, which gives leaky ReLU, and what makes it PReLU?

\(a_i = 0\) gives ReLU, and a small fixed value such as \(a_i = 0.01\) gives leaky ReLU. In PReLU \(a_i\) is a learnable parameter: backpropagation updates it along with the weights, so the negative-side slope changes during training.

Q4With \(\alpha = 1\), compute ELU and its derivative at \(x = -1\) and \(x = 2\), and compare with ReLU at \(x = -1\).

\(\operatorname{ELU}(-1) = e^{-1} - 1 \approx -0.632\) and \(\operatorname{ELU}'(-1) = e^{-1} \approx 0.368\); \(\operatorname{ELU}(2) = 2\) and \(\operatorname{ELU}'(2) = 1\). ReLU gives 0 and derivative 0 at \(x = -1\). ELU still passes a gradient and a negative output, which keeps the mean output near 0, at the cost of an exponential.

Q5Four residuals are \(1, -1, 2, 10\). Compute the total loss under \(\tfrac{1}{2}e^2\), \(\lvert e \rvert\) and Huber with \(\delta = 1\). What share does the outlier contribute, and how hard does it pull under each?

Squared: \(0.5 + 0.5 + 2 + 50 = 53\); the outlier contributes 94%. Absolute: \(1 + 1 + 2 + 10 = 14\); 71%. Huber: \(0.5 + 0.5 + 1.5 + 9.5 = 12\) (for \(\lvert e \rvert = 2\): \(1 \cdot 2 - 0.5\); for 10: \(10 - 0.5\)); 79%. The outlier's slope is 10 under MSE but 1 under MAE and Huber, so under MSE it pulls the fit ten times harder than under the other two.

Q6Compute the binary cross-entropy for \(y = 1\) with \(\hat y = 0.8\) and with \(\hat y = 0.2\), and for \(y = 0\) with \(\hat y = 0.8\).

\(y = 1\): \(-\ln 0.8 \approx 0.223\) and \(-\ln 0.2 \approx 1.609\). \(y = 0\), \(\hat y = 0.8\): \(-\ln(1 - 0.8) = -\ln 0.2 \approx 1.609\). A confident wrong answer costs about seven times as much as a fairly confident right one.

Q7Three output units receive scores \(z = (1, 2, 3)\) and the true class is the second. Compute the softmax probabilities, the prediction and the categorical cross-entropy.

\(e^z \approx (2.718, 7.389, 20.086)\), sum 30.193, so \(p \approx (0.090, 0.245, 0.665)\), which add up to 1. The prediction is the third class. With one-hot label \((0, 1, 0)\) the loss is \(-\ln 0.245 \approx 1.41\). Only the true class's probability enters it.

Q8Choose the output activation and the loss for: (a) predicting a house price, (b) spam or not spam, (c) a digit 0–9 with the labels stored as integers.

(a) Regression: a linear output with MSE, or MAE or Huber if the data has outliers. (b) Binary classification: one sigmoid output with binary cross-entropy. (c) Multi-class: ten softmax outputs with categorical cross-entropy on one-hot labels. With integer labels, Keras's sparse categorical cross-entropy computes the same loss (not in the video). In all three, the hidden layers use ReLU.
